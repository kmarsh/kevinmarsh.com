Every few weeks I get an email from my registrar reminding me that a domain is about to renew. I have [N] of them. Each email brings a small, specific pang of guilt: *oh right, that one.*

The last post here was about [overplayed](https://kevinmarsh.com/2023/09/28/overplayed/), where I wrote that I had "a ton more" I wanted to do with it and that you should keep checking back. That was three years ago. I think that tells you everything about how my side projects usually go.

## The Graveyard

If you're a solo developer with more ideas than evenings, you probably have a graveyard like mine. It's a folder of repos with names that seemed clever at 11pm. Most have a `README.md` that's just the project name, a Rails app that boots but doesn't do much, and a domain name pointing at nothing, or worse, at a parked page from the registrar.

For me the pattern is almost always the same. The first 80% is thrilling: the idea, the data model, the first time something actually works. The last 20% is where projects go to die. That's auth, billing, deploys, the settings page, the empty state, and the landing page that explains what the thing even is. Add a day job, kids, and a new shiny idea arriving right on schedule, and the project quietly slips into the folder.

The domains are cheap. What they cost is harder to measure. Each one is an open loop, a tiny tab left open in the back of my brain. Individually they're nothing. Collectively they're clutter.

## Activation Energy

What's changed for me over the last year is AI. It hasn't written my projects for me. What it has done is collapse the cost of *getting back in*.

Reopening a repo from 2019 used to cost an entire evening before I wrote a single line of code. I had to remember what I was doing and why, fight `bundle install` against three major Ruby versions of drift, and figure out which of the four half-finished branches was the real one. By the time things ran, the evening was gone and so was my motivation.

Now I can point an agent at the repo and say "read this, tell me what it does and where I left off, then get it running." That job used to take a night. Now it takes a coffee. It reminds me of getting [SongMeanings](https://kevinmarsh.com/2021/03/04/20-years-ago-songmeanings/) running again from an old CD-R. That took thirty minutes and felt like magic. Now that feeling is available on demand, for any project, any time.

It also chews through the boring 20% that used to kill everything. The login flow, the Stripe webhook, the deploy config and the little landing page aren't *hard*. They're just tedious enough that I'd never get to them. Handing those off means my limited evenings go to the parts I actually care about.

The result is that a few things have crawled out of the graveyard. Others I finally got a good look at and let go of, which turns out to be just as satisfying. Either way the loop closes and I get a tab back.

## The Double-Edged Sword

I've always kept a running list of ideas. Some are serious. Some are jokes that got a little too far along before I realized they were jokes.[^1] My brain never really puts them down. They come back to me in the shower, on a drive, or halfway through a meeting about something else entirely. For most of my life that list was a kind of pleasant torture, full of things I *could* build if I only had the time.

Now I kind of do. It's never been easier to take something off that list and make it real, joke ideas included. That's the good edge of the sword.

The other edge is that everyone else has the same list and the same superpower. My revived projects aren't launching into a quiet room. They're joining a flood of other people's weekend projects, each one shipped by someone and their agent in an evening or two. Every idea on my list, serious or silly, now launches into a very noisy space.

So the barrier isn't the code anymore. It's getting anyone to *care* about the thing you and your agent wrote. That part is still hard, and no amount of tokens seems to fix it. Distribution, attention, and a reason for a stranger to click are the new last 20%.

That's fine by me. Almost every one of my side projects started with a personal itch. I wanted to know how often radio stations play the same song, or I wanted a tool that worked the way my brain does. The first user was always me, and for a lot of these the only user will be me. If nobody else ever sees them, they've still done their job. They scratched the itch, they got crossed off the list, and the domain finally has something living on it.

For most of my life, the gap between "idea" and "Good Enough™" swallowed most of what I started. It's a lot narrower now. The world may not care, but at least A(I) shipped something.
